[SoilCheck](/work/soilcheck) answers one question each morning: which pots need checking today? Each plant gets a drying interval from what it is and what it's growing in, and the app sorts the garden by how close each pot is to drying out.

The obvious next step was weather. If it rained yesterday, the outdoor pots don't need water today. The National Weather Service publishes observations for free — no API key, no quota — so the plan was simple: pull recent rainfall near the user's ZIP code and push the schedule back automatically.

## The data doesn't say what you think it says

NWS precipitation observations are sparse. Stations report the field when it rains and leave it out when they have nothing to say. So a missing value doesn't mean "dry" — it means "unknown," and a schedule that treats it as dry will tell you to water a garden that's already soaked.

Then I compared five stations near Wilmington, NC. They disagreed on *how much* rain fell by up to 3×. But they agreed, consistently, on *whether* it rained.

<Callout>
**Good enough for a question, not for a decision.**
A 3× spread is fatal to an automatic adjustment — the schedule would swing on noise. It's perfectly fine for a prompt a person confirms.
</Callout>

## So the app asks

When a nearby station reports rain, SoilCheck shows a prompt:

> Rain reported nearby — a weather station on Tuesday recorded rain near you. Did your pots get wet?

You answer yes or no. The rain amount is shown for context and never fed into the maths. And the manual **It rained** button is always there, because you're standing in the garden and the weather station isn't.

## Temperature is different

Temperature is the exception, and the reason is the same data-quality argument run in the other direction. Every station reports temperature on every observation, so the app adjusts for it without asking: hot weather ahead shortens the interval, a cool spell lengthens it, and the card says which is happening.

It averages the next three days of forecast highs, so one hot afternoon doesn't whipsaw the whole garden. And it only applies to outdoor plants — the indoor ones live in your thermostat's climate.

## The general rule

Before automating a decision from someone else's data, check what that data is actually good at. Here it was the same API, two fields, and two opposite answers: one reliable enough to act on, one only reliable enough to ask about.
